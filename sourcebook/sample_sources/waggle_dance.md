# The waggle dance and its critics

When a forager finds a rich food source, she returns to the hive and performs a waggle dance on the vertical comb. The angle of the waggle run relative to vertical encodes the direction of the food relative to the sun. The duration of the waggle run encodes distance: longer runs mean farther food, with roughly one second of waggling corresponding to about one kilometre for many European colonies, although the exact calibration differs between populations.

## Does anyone actually follow the dance?

For decades some researchers argued that recruits find food mainly by smell, not by decoding the dance. The debate was largely settled by radar tracking studies, which followed individual recruits after they attended a dance. Most flew straight toward the location the dance indicated, then searched locally using odour. Dance information and scent therefore work together.

## How useful is the dance?

The dance is not equally valuable everywhere. Experiments that disrupted dance orientation, by making the comb horizontal and removing the sun's direction, found that colonies foraged less successfully in some habitats but barely suffered in others. The benefit appears largest where food is patchy and far away, and smallest where flowers are abundant and evenly spread near the hive.
