# How honeybees find their way

A foraging honeybee can fly several kilometres from its hive and return in a nearly straight line. It does this by combining three tools: a sun compass, a sense of distance travelled, and memory of landmarks near the hive.

## The sun compass

Bees use the position of the sun as their main reference direction. Because the sun moves across the sky during the day, a bee must correct for that movement, and experiments show that bees learn the sun's daily path for their own location. On cloudy days they rely on patterns of polarised light in patches of blue sky, which their eyes can detect through specialised photoreceptors in the upper rim of the eye.

## Measuring distance

Early researchers assumed bees measured distance by the energy they spent. Tunnel experiments in the 1990s and 2000s overturned this idea. When bees flew through a narrow tunnel lined with dense patterns, they overestimated distance dramatically, because the patterns streamed past their eyes quickly. This showed that bees estimate distance from optic flow: the amount of visual motion they experience along the way.

## Landmarks

Close to the hive, bees switch to landmark memory. They take learning flights when they first leave a new hive, turning back to face it and storing snapshots of the surroundings. Moving a prominent landmark can make returning bees search in the wrong place, which is strong evidence that they match stored views rather than following a map.
